# Abstract
Pleural mesothelioma (PM) is a highly aggressive malignancy linked to asbestos exposure and characterized by marked histological and molecular heterogeneity, particularly in biphasic tumors that contain both epithelioid and sarcomatoid components. While prior genomic and transcriptomic studies suggest that PM subtypes exist along a molecular continuum, the spatial organization of tumor states and their interactions with the tumor microenvironment (TME) remain poorly defined.

Here, we integrate single-cell-resolved spatial transcriptomics, trajectory inference, copy number variation (CNV), and multi-platform validation to characterize tumor state heterogeneity in PM. Using Xenium Prime profiling of a whole-slide biphasic PM resection, we identify four transcriptionally and spatially distinct tumor states arranged in a radial architecture, including intermediate populations enriched for proliferation and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) associated programs. These states appear ordered along a continuous transcriptional axis aligned with spatial organization and gradual changes in tumor cell morphology, while copy number phylogeny based on haplotype tracing indicates a shared malignant clone across epithelioid and sarcomatoid states.

We demonstrate that these tumor state programs are reproducible across additional patients profiled using Xenium Prime tissue microarrays and are further recovered in a 23-patient cohort analyzed using the GeoMx™ Digital Spatial Profiler Whole Transcriptome Atlas assay. Inferred cell-cell communication analysis reveals subtype-specific TME signaling, including EMT-associated AXL expression along with immune modulatory CD70 expression in biphasic tumors and VEGFR2 angiogenic signaling in epithelioid tumors. 

Together, these findings reveal a novel EMT-associated cellular-state transition niche in PM and the associated TME programs, providing a spatially resolved framework for understanding how intra- and inter-cellular plasticity drives tumor heterogeneity. This work establishes a pathomics foundation for future functional studies and suggests that spatial molecular profiling may aid in delineating PM patient heterogeneity and personalizing treatment.
